The problem

Sometimes you know, before seeing any data, that certain correlations are exactly zero — a graphical structure, a confirmatory factor model, a physical constraint. You cannot just sample a correlation matrix from an LKJ prior and zero out cells: the result is generally not positive definite. The zeros have to be built into the parameterization itself.

This post races twelve Stan parameterizations of “LKJ prior + hard zeros” against each other. All twelve target the same posterior (verified against each other within Monte Carlo error), so the comparison is purely about sampling geometry: divergences, effective sample size, and cost per gradient evaluation.

The punchline: the two families of solutions make opposite trade-offs, and a hybrid — an exact-constraint subspace construction with a canonical triangular basis — gets the best of both: zero divergences across every run and the best ESS per gradient in the field.

Family 1: forced zeros

The construction I first saw from Bob Carpenter walks the Cholesky factor row by row. Writing \(\Omega = L L'\) with unit-norm rows of \(L\), the correlation in cell \((i,j)\) is

\[ \Omega_{ij} = b_1 + L_{ij} L_{jj}, \qquad b_1 = \sum_{k<j} L_{ik} L_{jk}, \]

so a structural zero pins \(L_{ij} = -b_1 / L_{jj}\) — no parameter needed. Conditioning the LKJ distribution on \(\Omega_{ij} = 0\) (a delta function in \(\Omega\) coordinates) contributes a density correction \(-\log L_{jj}\) per zero.

if (is_zero(i, j, zeros, zero_idx)) {
  real b = dot_product(L[j, 1:(j - 1)], L[i, 1:(j - 1)]);
  L[i, j] = -b / L[j, j];   // implies Omega[i, j] == 0
  jacobian += -log(L[j, j]);
  zero_idx += 1;
}

The forced-zero trick composes with essentially any Cholesky-style parameterization of the free cells. I tested eight members of this family:

label free-cell parameterization
carpenter direct Cholesky entries, bounded by the remaining stick
pinkney correlation-scale value divided by \(L_{jj}\), multiplicative stick
ldl / ldl2 / ldl3 \(LDL'\) factorization; stick tracked by log_diff_exp, log1m-ratio, or natural scale
cvine C-vine partial correlations with the LKJ Beta factorization
rowscale direct Cholesky entries, row-scaling stick
schur canonical partial correlations, \(L_{ij} = r \tanh(x)\)

They all share one flaw. The forced value \(-b_1/L_{jj}\) can exceed the row’s remaining stick, \(\sqrt{1 - \sum_{k<j} L_{ik}^2}\). When it does, a downstream sqrt of a negative number produces NaN, the proposal is rejected, and near that boundary the sampler diverges. Every member of the family runs at roughly a 1% divergence rate on the test patterns below — a bias risk you have to manage with adapt_delta forever.

The three LDL variants deserve a footnote: they define identical densities and differ only in floating-point arithmetic, yet their divergence totals span 356–466. That spread is a direct measurement of the noise floor for single-fit divergence counts, and it is wider than most gaps between genuinely different parameterizations. Beware of divergence league tables built from single runs.

Family 2: exact-constraint subspaces

The second idea eliminates the boundary entirely. I learned it from Seth Axen, in a Turing.jl discussion on positive definite matrices with structural zeros:

For each column j, each column i for i < j that is orthogonal to column j decreases the dimensionality of the hemisphere by 1. So we simply construct a point on a lower dimensional hemisphere and use the QR decomposition of the orthogonal columns to lift it to a higher dimensional hemisphere.

Row \(i\) of a correlation Cholesky factor is a unit vector with positive last coordinate. Because row \(j\) is zero beyond column \(j\), the constraint \(\Omega_{ij}=0\) says row \(i\) is orthogonal to row \(j\) as a whole vector. So a row with \(n_z\) zeros lives on a unit hemisphere inside the orthogonal complement of its zero-partner rows — a sphere of dimension \(d = i - 1 - n_z\). Sample there and the constraint holds by construction: no proposal can ever be infeasible, so the divergence mechanism of Family 1 does not exist. The joint delta over a row’s zeros contributes \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det(A'A)\), where \(A\) collects the zero-partner rows — Axen’s “divide by the determinant of the R factor.”

My first two implementations (qr, qr2) followed the recipe literally: Householder QR of \(A\), complement basis from the full \(Q\), hemisphere chart via a gnomonic map or tanh sticks. They achieved exactly zero divergences — and about 40% of the ESS of the forced-zero family. The chart didn’t matter; the basis did. Householder’s complement columns are an arbitrary orthonormal frame that spins as the earlier rows move, so the raw coordinates keep changing meaning underneath the sampler.

The fix: a canonical triangular basis (tri)

Replace the QR basis with one anchored to the matrix entries. For each free column \(k\) of row \(i\), take \(e_k\) and fill in the row’s forced columns by back-substitution through the zero constraints — Carpenter’s fill applied to basis vectors instead of the sampled row. Collect these as the columns of \(T\). Splitting coordinates into forced (\(Z\)) and free (\(F\)) sets, \(T_F = I\) and \(T_Z = -(A_Z')^{-1} A_F'\), and orthonormalizing costs one small Cholesky: with \(S = \operatorname{chol}(T'T)\), the map \(v = T S'^{-T} z\) satisfies \(\lVert v \rVert = \lVert z \rVert\), so the sphere-measure bookkeeping is unchanged.

Two pleasant identities finish the job. First, because the zero-partner rows have triangular support, \(A_Z\) is triangular with the partners’ diagonals on its diagonal, and Sylvester’s determinant identity gives

\[ \det(A'A) \;=\; \Big(\prod_{j \in Z} L_{jj}\Big)^{2} \, \det(T'T), \]

so the delta term splits into Carpenter’s \(-\sum_j \log L_{jj}\) — which now falls out analytically, no linear algebra required — plus \(-\sum_k \log S_{kk}\), which rides along free with the orthonormalization Cholesky. Second, since \(T_F = I\), the Gram is \(T'T = I + W'W\) with \(W\) just the \(n_z\) forced-coordinate rows, so the crossprod works on length-\(n_z\) vectors. Per constrained row, the entire construction costs one \(d \times d\) Cholesky and some small matrix-vector products. No QR anywhere.

unitvec-tri-corr-zeros.stan
functions {
  matrix cholesky_corr_constrain_tri_jacobian(int K, vector raw,
                                              array[,] int zeros) {
    matrix[K, K] L = rep_matrix(0, K, K);
    int raw_idx = 1;
    int zero_idx = 1;
    L[1, 1] = 1;

    for (i in 2:K) {
      int n_z = 0;
      while (zero_idx + n_z <= size(zeros)
             && zeros[zero_idx + n_z, 1] == i) {
        n_z += 1;
      }
      int d = i - 1 - n_z;

      // subsphere coordinates via tanh sticks (cosh-form jacobian)
      vector[d] z;
      real r = 1;
      for (k in 1:d) {
        real x = raw[raw_idx];
        real cosh_x = cosh(x);
        z[k] = r * tanh(x);
        jacobian += -(d - k + 2) * log(cosh_x);
        r /= cosh_x;
        raw_idx += 1;
      }

      if (n_z == 0) {
        if (d > 0) {
          L[i, 1:d] = z';
        }
        L[i, i] = r;
      } else {
        array[n_z] int zc;
        for (m in 1:n_z) {
          zc[m] = zeros[zero_idx + m - 1, 2];
        }

        // the joint-delta term -0.5 * log det(Gram of zero-partner rows)
        // factors: det(A'A) = prod(L[j,j])^2 * det(T'T) by Sylvester
        for (m in 1:n_z) {
          jacobian += -log(L[zc[m], zc[m]]);
        }

        if (d > 0) {
          array[d] int free_cols;
          {
            int fc = 0;
            int zp = 1;
            for (c in 1:(i - 1)) {
              if (zp <= n_z && zc[zp] == c) {
                zp += 1;
              } else {
                fc += 1;
                free_cols[fc] = c;
              }
            }
          }
          // triangular basis: unit at each free column, forced columns
          // filled by back-substitution so every column is orthogonal to
          // the zero-partner rows
          matrix[i - 1, d] T = rep_matrix(0, i - 1, d);
          for (k in 1:d) {
            int col = free_cols[k];
            T[col, k] = 1;
            for (m in 1:n_z) {
              int j = zc[m];
              if (j > col) {
                T[j, k] = -dot_product(L[j, 1:(j - 1)], T[1:(j - 1), k])
                          / L[j, j];
              }
            }
          }
          // T's free-column rows are the identity, so T'T = I + W'W with
          // W the forced-column rows only
          matrix[n_z, d] W;
          for (m in 1:n_z) {
            W[m, ] = T[zc[m], ];
          }
          matrix[d, d] S = cholesky_decompose(add_diag(crossprod(W), 1.0));
          jacobian += -sum(log(diagonal(S)));
          vector[d] t = mdivide_right_tri_low(z', S)';
          L[i, 1:(i - 1)] = (T * t)';
        }
        L[i, i] = r;
        zero_idx += n_z;
      }
    }
    return L;
  }
}
data {
  int<lower=2> K;
  real<lower=0> eta;
  int<lower=0, upper=choose(K, 2)> N_zero;
  array[N_zero, 2] int zeros;  // lower triangular, row major order
}
parameters {
  vector[choose(K, 2) - N_zero] raw;
}
transformed parameters {
  matrix[K, K] L_Omega = cholesky_corr_constrain_tri_jacobian(K, raw, zeros);
}
model {
  L_Omega ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(eta);
}
generated quantities {
  matrix[K, K] Omega = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Omega);
}

I also tried a “donut” variant — one extra parameter per row, normalizing a free vector instead of using a compact chart. It kept the exact-constraint geometry but the \(|u_{\text{last}}|\) fold put a power-law barrier at the equator and it diverged worse than anything else in the field (2,531 total). Not every overparameterization trick transfers.

Results

Setup: \(K = 7\), \(\eta = 4\), prior-only, five random zero patterns (about half of the 21 lower-triangular cells zeroed, seeds 1–5), 4 chains \(\times\) 2000 draws after 1000 warmup, default adapt_delta = 0.8, CmdStan 2.39. Correctness was checked on a separate pattern: all twelve posteriors match the carpenter baseline within Monte Carlo error (max free-cell mean difference \(\le 0.008\) at bulk ESS \(\ge\) 3,700), and the constrained cells are numerically exact zeros (\(\sim 10^{-19}\)).

The tri column is the whole story: zero divergences on every pattern, the highest median ESS in the table, the best ESS per gradient evaluation, and the lowest wall-clock time. The subspace family’s short trajectories (no boundary to fight) plus a well-anchored basis means it wins on every axis at once — the usual robustness-versus-speed trade simply dissolves.

Caveats

  • Everything above is \(K = 7\), \(\eta = 4\), prior-only, dense zero patterns. Larger \(K\), data likelihoods, and sparse patterns may reorder the forced-zero family — though the structural argument for tri (no infeasible region exists) is dimension-independent.
  • Divergence counts from single fits are noisy; the identical-density LDL trio spans 356–466 on the same patterns. The forced-vs-subspace gap (hundreds vs. exactly zero) is far outside that noise; the within-family rankings are not.
  • The tri construction needs the zero list sorted in row-major order and one \(d \times d\) cholesky_decompose per constrained row per gradient — negligible at this size, worth profiling at \(K \gtrsim 50\).

Attribution

The exact-constraint subspace idea — zeros as orthogonality constraints, sampling on a lower-dimensional hemisphere and lifting through the orthogonal complement, with the \(\det R\) Jacobian factor — is due to Seth Axen, from TuringLang/Turing.jl discussion #2067. The tri model is that idea with a different (canonical, triangular) choice of complement basis and the determinant factored analytically.

The forced-zero construction and its \(-\log L_{jj}\) conditioning correction I learned from Bob Carpenter. The bounded/constrained Cholesky machinery used by several of the Family-1 variants is described in my short note on flexible Cholesky parameterizations.
